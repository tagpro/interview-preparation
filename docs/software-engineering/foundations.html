Part one

Numbers and laws

Three quantitative tools do most of the work in backend design. Knowing them turns "it feels slow" into "we are at 85% utilisation on a single-threaded resource, so the wait is six times the service time".

Foundations deep · 01

The latency scale you argue from

Every design argument is really a claim about which of these numbers you are paying. They span nine orders of magnitude, so intuition fails — you have to know roughly where each operation sits.

Foundations deep · 02

Little’s Law: the only capacity formula you need

Work in progress equals arrival rate times time in system: L = λW. It holds for any stable system, with no assumptions about distributions. Thread pools, connection pools, queue depths and pod counts all fall out of it.

Three questions Little’s Law answers immediately

How many workers? Throughput target × per-item time. 500 msg/s at 200 ms each needs 100 concurrent consumers. How deep is my queue allowed to be? Depth ÷ drain rate = added latency; a 10,000-item queue draining at 500/s is 20 seconds of delay, so if your SLO is 2s the bound is 1,000. Why did connections run out? Because W grew, not because λ did.

Foundations deep · 03

Why systems fall over at 80%, not 100%

Waiting time does not grow linearly with load. For a single queued resource the wait multiplier is 1/(1−ρ), which is flat and forgiving until it is neither. Capacity planning is really about staying left of the knee.

Part two

Concurrency, in depth

Two things go wrong with concurrent code: threads disagree about what memory says, and threads queue behind each other. The first is a correctness problem with a precise rule; the second is a scaling problem with a precise curve.

Foundations deep · 04

Memory visibility and the happens-before edge

A write by one thread is not automatically a write the others can see. Compilers reorder, CPUs buffer, caches lag. Correctness comes from establishing an edge — a synchronising operation that both sides participate in.

Foundations deep · 05

Contention, coherency, and the curve that turns down

Adding workers buys less than you expect (Amdahl) and eventually buys negative throughput (the Universal Scalability Law). The second effect surprises people, and it is the reason a bigger instance sometimes performs worse.

Symptoms you can measure

Throughput flat while CPU climbs → contention. Throughput falling as you add pods or threads → coherency: you are paying more to coordinate than you gain. Lock wait time and context-switch rate are the two counters that separate them.

The fix is structural, not tuning

Shrink the critical section, shard the lock by key, or remove the sharing entirely with a single-writer-per-partition design. Faster locks buy you a constant factor; removing the shared point changes the shape of the curve.

Part three

Events, in depth

Asynchronous systems trade one hard problem (availability coupling) for two others: what "delivered" means, and what happens when step three of five fails after steps one and two already committed.

Foundations deep · 06

Delivery semantics are just where you put the ack

There is no magic in "exactly once". The three semantics differ only in the order of two operations — acknowledging the message and doing the work — plus, optionally, a record of what you have already done.

Foundations deep · 07

Sagas: what happens when step three fails

A distributed transaction cannot roll back — steps one and two are already committed in other systems. A saga replaces rollback with compensation: a new, forward action that undoes the business effect of an earlier one.

Part four

Caching, in depth

Two things are worth knowing beyond "store the answer": the arithmetic that tells you what a hit ratio is actually worth, and the exact interleavings that leave a wrong value sitting in the cache.

Foundations deep · 08

The arithmetic of a hit ratio

Effective latency is h · Lhit + (1−h) · Lmiss, and origin load is (1−h) · λ. Both are dominated by the miss term, which is why the last percent of hit ratio is worth more than the first ninety.

Foundations deep · 09

The invalidation race, drawn

Cache-aside has one interleaving that quietly stores a value that was already stale when it was read. It is rare per request and certain at scale, and no amount of careful ordering in the writer removes it.

Part five

Storage, in depth

Every storage engine spends the same three currencies: read work, write work, and space. You cannot minimise all three, and knowing which one a design is spending explains most of its behaviour under load.

Foundations deep · 10

Read, update, memory — pick two

Amplification is the ratio between the work you asked for and the work the disk actually did. A B-tree pays it on writes; an LSM pays it on reads and on background compaction; an index pays it in space and on every write to the table.

The index count is a write-throughput decision

Each secondary index is another structure to update on every insert and on any update touching its columns. Six indexes on a hot table means one logical write becomes seven physical ones, at random offsets. When write latency creeps up with no code change, count the indexes that were added "just for that one report".

Part six

Load and failure, in depth

Three mechanisms decide whether an overloaded system degrades or collapses: whether pressure propagates backwards, whether deadlines shrink as they travel, and whether the failure sustains itself after the trigger is gone.

Foundations deep · 11

Backpressure, drawn end to end

Backpressure is the property that a slow component makes its caller slow down, all the way to the entry point. Its absence is not neutral — unbounded buffers convert a throughput shortfall into unbounded latency and then into an out-of-memory kill.

Foundations deep · 12

Timeout budgets and availability arithmetic

Two numbers travel down a call tree and must be managed explicitly: the time remaining, which shrinks at every hop, and the probability of success, which multiplies at every hop.

Foundations deep · 13

Metastable failure: when removing the cause doesn’t help

The hardest outages have this shape: a trigger pushes the system into a state that sustains itself, so the system stays down after the trigger is gone. Adding capacity does not fix it, and restarting often makes it worse.

Part seven

Put it together

One worked example that uses every tool on this page. This is the shape of answer a systems-design interview is looking for, and the shape of a capacity document that survives review.

Foundations deep · 14

Worked example: size this service

The brief: an order-read API, 3,000 requests per second at peak, p50 of 40 ms and p99 of 120 ms, backed by Postgres and a Redis cache. How many pods, how many connections, what timeouts, and what breaks first?

  1. Start from throughput and latency, never from instance sizes. Concurrency is derived, not chosen.
  2. Pick a utilisation target before counting boxes — 60–70% for latency-sensitive work, higher only for batch where queueing is free.
  3. Find the scarcest serialised resource. Connections, partitions, a single-writer shard, a third-party rate limit. That is your real capacity, whatever CPU says.
  4. Survive N-1. Size so the remaining zones absorb the load; otherwise an AZ failure is an outage with extra steps.
  5. Give every queue a bound and every wait a timeout, then decide what the system does when it hits them — degrade, shed, or fail.
  6. Write down what breaks first and at what number. An estimate you can falsify is worth more than a precise one you cannot.

The four questions, restated for this page

What is the queue? Every stage has one, even when it is only a connection pool. How full is it? Utilisation, not capacity, sets latency. What happens when the thing behind it slows down? In-flight work grows by Little's Law until something bounds it. Does the failure sustain itself? If retries and cold caches can feed it, you need a shedding lever, not a bigger cluster.

How this page differs from the overview. The ladder page names the mechanisms and sorts them by depth. This one gives you the arithmetic — the formulas, the curves, and the exact interleavings — so that "we should add a cache" becomes "at h = 95% the origin still sees 500 rps, and we are sized for 200".

All diagrams are hand-drawn SVG. Every number is either a definition, a standard queueing result, or a well-known order of magnitude you can verify with a benchmark.
